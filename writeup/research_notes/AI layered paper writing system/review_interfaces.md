# Review interfaces and workflows for AI-proposed edits to a long document (LaTeX + git + Overleaf)

Research date: 2026-10-28. Source dates are given inline. Ink & Switch notebook pages and the ACM DL full text for the CHI 2026 papers are JavaScript-rendered or paywalled (403), so claims marked "(search snippet)" come from the search engine's extract of that page, not a full read.

## Q1. What does research on human-AI co-writing and editing interfaces say about how AI edits should be presented for review?

### Takeaway
The strongest controlled evidence (InkSync, Salesforce/Berkeley, Sept 2023; AnchoredAI, Sept 2025) favours presenting AI output as small, localized, executable edits anchored to the text over chat. Executable edits were faster than a chat that did not apply them. They also produced fewer factual errors than conversational modes, and typed "this edit adds new information" warnings with a verify step roughly doubled the share of planted inaccurate edits that reviewers caught. Anchoring cuts wholesale pasting and raises ownership, but it also raises workload. So friction should go only to edits that change claims. Ink & Switch's Patchwork (2024) treats AI edits as a branch that the author partially merges, with each change explained by citing a rule. It also found this "too heavy" for many small edits.

### Cited Findings
**Ink & Switch / Patchwork (universal version control)**
- Patchwork started at Ink & Switch in January 2024 (Litt, Sonnentag, Schöning, Wiggins, van Hardenberg, Henry). Its goal is "universal version control", defined as "a set of conceptual primitives that can support simple and powerful versioning for all kinds of apps, across all kinds of collaboration patterns." (newsletter dated 5 May 2024) — [Litt, "Towards universal version control with Patchwork"](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Litt's argument: "Just like human collaborators, AI tools need a way to stage tentative changes for others to review." — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Prototype: an AI bot proposes a branch of suggestions that the user accepts or rejects, just like a human branch. Litt built a bot that edits to the lab's style guide and **explains each change by citing that guide**. — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Litt also says this branch-review workflow is "too heavy" in many cases. AI is always on and faster than humans, which suggests lighter modes such as Copilot-style ghost text. — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- AI fills in branch names and edit descriptions by default. Lesson: AI works well when it handles "the boring meta-work". — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- "Edit Groups" groups sets of edits into reviewable units, with a UI for grouping and commenting on recent edits. Chat messages are embedded in version history to make history "legible and discussable". — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Existing prose diff views "are often very noisy, making it hard to review (e.g. suggested edits in Google Docs)" (search snippet). — [Ink & Switch Patchwork notebook 02, "Writing prose"](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/02)
- The AI style-guide bot "makes changes as another collaborator in the document. It puts changes on a branch, which you can choose to partially or completely merge". Changes are highlighted in a diff, and history shows which edits came from the bot (search snippet). — [Ink & Switch Patchwork notebook 07, "AI bots in version control"](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/07/)

**InkSync / "Beyond the Chat: Executable and Verifiable Text-Editing with LLMs"** (Laban, Vig, Hearst, Xiong, Wu; arXiv 2309.15337, 27 Sept 2023)
- Each suggestion is an executable, underlined in-document edit stored as a JSON object: original text, replacement text, source component, and a flag for whether it **adds new information**. Components: Chat, Comment (localized), Markers (background checks), Brainstorm. — [arXiv 2309.15337](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15337v1)
- Study 1 (N=52, within-subjects, 6-min sessions): non-executable chat was the slowest editing condition, and all executable-edit interfaces were more efficient than manual editing. Remaining typos or informal phrases: Manual 12.9, non-executable chat 12.8, Markers 7.0, Chat 11.6, all four components 5.2. — [arXiv 2309.15337](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15337v1)
- **Major factual errors appeared in 14.8% of documents from conversational conditions vs 6% from non-conversational ones.** — [arXiv 2309.15337](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15337v1)
- Study 2 (N=35, with planted inaccurate edits): Warn + Verify raised the share of inaccurate edits prevented from 23.4% to 43.5% (the "44% reduction" headline). The audit view identified up to 73% of inaccurate edits. Verifications took 44 s on average, and 80% finished within a minute. The new-information flag agreed with annotators 97.5% of the time on 200 edits. — [arXiv 2309.15337](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15337v1)

**AnchoredAI** (Lou, Crowley, Dodson, Yoon; arXiv 2509.16128, 19 Sept 2025; no venue stated)
- AI comments are anchored to text spans in a Word add-in (GPT-4o). Anchors survive edits by diffing document versions and re-localizing. If an anchor string is not unique, it is widened from word to sentence to paragraph to section. — [arXiv 2509.16128](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16128v1)
- N=22 students, within-subjects, chat vs anchored. On local revision tasks, words pasted per paste were chat 199.1 vs anchored 35.2 (p<.001), and the share of the document changed was 22.5% vs 6.8% (p=.001). On global (structure) tasks the share changed did not differ significantly. — [arXiv 2509.16128](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16128v1)
- Ownership "I am the main contributor" was 4.0 anchored vs 2.3 chat (p<.001), and control 4.1 vs 3.5 (p=.016). **Workload was higher with anchoring**: NASA-TLX mental demand 80.7 vs 51.8, effort 73.0 vs 41.4 (both p<.001). — [arXiv 2509.16128](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16128v1)
- Design implications: "modest friction" supports authorship but costs effort. Anchoring suits precise local edits, and chat may suit broad restructuring. Use familiar metaphors (comments, tracked changes, suggestions). Overreliance was not measured directly. — [arXiv 2509.16128](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16128v1)

**Other co-writing studies**
- CoAuthor (Lee, Liang, Yang; CHI '22, Apr 29–May 5 2022): 63 writers, four GPT-3 instances, 1,445 sessions, with a public replay tool. — [arXiv 2201.06796](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.06796). Stanford HAI summary: the best collaborations occurred "when the writer uses his or her own creative sensibilities to evaluate the suggestions and decides what to keep and what to leave out." — [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/meet-coauthor-experiment-human-ai-collaborative-writing)
- Wordcraft (Yuan et al., IUI '22): an editor with LLM controls (story seeds, continuation, elaborate selection) built by few-shot prompting LaMDA. Writers often wanted to define their own controls on the fly. A follow-up with professional writers (Ippolito, Yuan et al., arXiv 2211.05030, Nov 2022) reported that the UI "matters as much as the underlying language model". — [arXiv 2211.05030](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.05030); [Wordcraft project page](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/cross_fac/eduport/edufund/projects/yang/projects/wordcraft-a-human-ai-collaborative-editor-for-story-writing/)
- Script&Shift (arXiv 2506.20595, June 2025), an RCT: a process-oriented tool gave more writer agency than chat. The authors attribute this to chat generating full text, which "encourages passive acceptance" (search snippet). — [arXiv 2506.20595](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20595v1)
- "Reactive Writers" (ACM, CHI 2026 DOI series): inline suggestions at the cursor pulled writers' attention away from their own ideation (search snippet). — [ACM 10.1145/3772318.3791529](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3791529)
- "When Help Hurts: Verification Load and Fatigue with AI Coding Assistants" (ACM, CHI 2026 DOI series; code, not prose): inline, chat and structured interfaces trade off assistance against verification burden. Inline had higher acceptance on simple changes (search snippet; full text returned 403). — [ACM 10.1145/3772318.3791176](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3791176)
- CHI 2025 (Cornell): autocomplete-style suggestions shifted writing toward Western styles. Indian participants had to correct more suggestions, which reduced their productivity gains. — [Cornell Chronicle, Apr 2025](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/04/ai-suggestions-make-writing-more-generic-western)

**Automation bias / overreliance**
- AIES paper: overreliance framed as misaligned trust, with automation bias and complacency bias as candidate mechanisms. — [AIES (ojs.aaai.org)](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/download/43225/50785/47331)
- In LLM-assisted software development, 29.46% (238/808) of LLM-related actions were later reversed. — [arXiv 2601.08045, Jan 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08045)
- Values-framing study (N=149, India/US): framing the AI as value-laden as a lever to reduce reliance on writing suggestions. — [arXiv 2605.20512, May 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20512)

### Inferences
- The InkSync result has a direct analogue for this paper. Its "adds new information" flag maps onto a typed edit class: **changes a number, claim, citation or hedge** vs **wording only**. Verification effort (around 44 s per flagged edit in InkSync) should go only to the first class. Wording edits can be bulk-accepted.
- Chat-style "here is the rewritten section" output is the worst format for review. Two studies (InkSync, AnchoredAI) tie it to more wholesale acceptance and, in InkSync, to more factual errors. Agents should emit span-level edits with an anchor (section and `\label`/sentence ID), not rewritten sections.
- The workload cost in AnchoredAI is the price of engagement. For an expert sole author who wants minimal time, the design goal is *selective* friction: high friction on the few claim-changing edits, near-zero on mechanical ones.
- Patchwork's "explain each change by citing the style guide" is the cheapest rationale format. Each AI edit cites a rule ID (from a house style file or a reviewer comment ID) instead of free prose.
- Patchwork's "too heavy" finding suggests two lanes. A branch/PR with per-edit review is for substantive changes. An auto-applied, attributed, revertible lane covers mechanical fixes (typos, macro renames, sembr reflow), with a log the author can scan.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly compares tracked-changes presentation with chat for AI edits to **long academic documents**. InkSync used short sales emails and AnchoredAI used student essays.
- No study found that measures review fatigue over a long sequence of AI prose edits (acceptance rate drift, missed errors late in a session). The CHI 2026 "When Help Hurts" paper is the closest, but it is about code and only its abstract-level snippet was available.
- PaperWeaver was not retrieved. From memory (unverified): it is a CHI 2024 system by Lee et al. for contextualizing recommended papers in topical alerts, not an editing or review interface. Verify before citing.
- The original Wordcraft IUI 2022 user-study results and CoAuthor's acceptance-rate figures were not retrieved.
- No first-party documentation was retrieved for the Lex editor or Google Docs suggestion mode.

## Q2. What diff and review tooling exists for prose and LaTeX, and how well does it support accept/edit/reject?

### Takeaway
Line-based diffs are poorly matched to wrapped prose. Semantic line breaks (one clause or sentence per line) plus `git diff --word-diff` make each AI edit a one-line, attributable hunk. latexdiff gives a rendered PDF redline but only all-or-nothing accept/reject (latexrevise). Manubot shows that GitHub pull requests plus CI checks work as a review venue for papers. Commercial AI editors are converging on tracked changes for AI edits (Word Copilot) and per-hunk accept/reject (Cursor), but defaults are drifting toward session-level bulk review, and users push back on that.

### Cited Findings
- Semantic Line Breaks spec: put line breaks at idea or clause boundaries so that line breaks act as semantic delimiters. The spec notes that default Git diff options "emphasize line changes in a way that may obscure certain revisions" and recommends `git diff --word-diff`. — [sembr specification (GitHub)](https://github.com/sembr/specification)
- Practitioner example: changing "10 ns" to "20 ns" in a hard-wrapped paragraph marks every reflowed line as modified, while with semantic breaks "Git highlights exactly one line." — [semantic line breaks blog](https://turtletech-ehf.pages.dev/posts/semantic-line-breaks-snapper/)
- Counterpoint: in the Python docs discussion, one participant argues that diff readability is a diff-tool problem, not an authoring one. Another says sembr works "especially if every collaborator in a project is on board." — [discuss.python.org thread](https://discuss.python.org/t/semantic-line-breaks/13874)
- latexdiff (CTAN version 2026-01-02, announced 2026-01-07; maintainer F. J. Tilmann; in TeX Live and MiKTeX) marks up differences between two LaTeX files for visual review. Its companion `latexrevise` is "a rudimentary revision facility" that accepts or rejects **all** changes at once. Selective acceptance requires hand-editing the diff file. — [CTAN latexdiff](https://ctan.org/pkg/latexdiff)
- Manubot ("Open collaborative writing with Manubot", Himmelstein et al., PLOS Comput Biol, 2019-06-24, DOI 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1007128): manuscripts are plain-text Markdown in GitHub. Contributors open pull requests, CI tests whether the change breaks the build and detects common errors such as invalid citations, and maintainers approve and merge. — [greenelab/meta-review repo](https://github.com/greenelab/meta-review); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pcbi00/1007128.html)
- Manubot's Deep Review attracted 36 authors from 20 institutions who were not chosen in advance, with authorship tied to substantive pull requests. — [Nature TechBlog](https://blogs.nature.com/blog/techblog-manubot-powers-a-crowdsourced-deep-learning-review/)
- Word: Accept makes a tracked change permanent, Reject removes it, Previous/Next steps through changes, and bulk options sit under the Accept/Reject arrows. — [Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/training/accept-tracked-changes)
- Word Copilot (secondary sources, unverified against a Microsoft announcement): Copilot's edits now appear as tracked changes that can be accepted or rejected individually, at "word-level precision" by default, with contextual comments explaining them. — [collab365](https://spaces.collab365.com/posts/microsoft-adds-track-changes-to-copilot-in-word-eKAB39). An SEO-style guide reports that Copilot can split one suggestion into several tracked entries, which makes review harder. — [WiseChecker](https://wisechecker.com/?p=30447)
- Cursor: per-change Accept/Reject still exists, but the Agent Window default shifted to session-level review. Users report that Cursor 3 auto-applies agent changes and offers a "Discard All Changes" button. — [Cursor forum: bring back per-change Apply](https://forum.cursor.com/t/bring-back-per-change-apply-inline-diff-review-you-re-throwing-away-your-best-ux-advantage/160856); [Cursor forum: auto-accept](https://forum.cursor.com/t/cursor-keeps-auto-accepting-its-own-changes/152233)
- Users ask to review agent edits "in page order" in Cursor, and to see all files with pending AI edits. — [Cursor forum: page order](https://forum.cursor.com/t/can-you-review-agent-edits-in-page-order/171421); [Cursor forum: stale edits](https://forum.cursor.com/t/stale-ai-edits-persist-across-sessions-with-no-way-to-find-or-bulk-discard-them/148384)
- A Claude Code feature request contrasts Claude Code's all-or-nothing edit acceptance in the VS Code extension with Cursor's per-hunk accept/reject. — [anthropics/claude-code issue #42448](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/42448)
- Prose diffs in Google Docs suggestion mode are "often very noisy" (Ink & Switch, search snippet). — [Ink & Switch notebook 02](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/02)

### Inferences
- **Source format.** Adopt one-sentence-per-line (sembr) in the `.tex` body. Combined with the existing generated number macros, an AI edit that changes a reported number touches only the macro file, and a wording edit touches one line. Numeric changes and prose changes then separate structurally at the git level.
- **Two diff renderings per review unit.** (a) `git diff --word-diff=color` (or `--word-diff=plain` in Markdown) for the terminal or GitHub. (b) A latexdiff PDF of only the changed section, for reading in typeset form. Known latexdiff features not verified in this pass: `latexdiff-vc --git -r <rev>` for git revisions, `--flatten` for `\input` trees, `--math-markup`. Check the manual.
- **Accept/reject granularity.** latexrevise is all-or-nothing, so per-edit decisions are best done in git: one commit per review unit on a branch, so that "reject" means drop or revert that commit and "accept" means keep it. The latexdiff PDF is for reading, not for deciding.
- **Overleaf as the comment surface.** The user already reads in Overleaf. A practical pattern is to push the review branch's typeset redline (or the layer documents) to Overleaf and use Overleaf comments only for *edit* decisions, which agents then pull back. Accept and reject can be recorded as one-token replies (e.g. `A`, `R`, `E: <text>`) keyed by card ID.
- The Cursor complaints show two things expert reviewers want: per-unit decisions, and review **in document order** rather than generation order. Both are cheap to provide in a generated review packet.

### Gaps
- Overleaf's track-changes, comments and history-labels documentation was not retrieved. The current plan availability of track changes, and whether history labels can mark AI-proposal checkpoints, are unverified.
- Lex's AI-edit UX and Google Docs suggestion-mode behaviour with AI (Gemini) were not retrieved.
- No quantitative study was found comparing word-diff, latexdiff PDF and track-changes for reviewer time or accuracy on prose.

## Q3. How should one change be packaged with its rationale, evidence and checks so a reviewer can decide in seconds? (decision card / change brief)

### Takeaway
Software code review has the most mature evidence. A review unit should be one self-contained change: Google's guidance is about 100 lines as reasonable and 1,000 as too large, and the observed median at Google is about 24 lines (secondary sources). Refactors should be split from behaviour changes, and stacking keeps the author unblocked. Defect detection falls off beyond roughly 200–400 lines per session or 300–500 lines per hour (a vendor study, 2006). For descriptions, an MSR 2026 study of about 80K PRs finds that *stating what kind of feedback is wanted* is the element most associated with acceptance and reviewer engagement. InkSync's structured edit record (original, replacement, source, new-info flag), Patchwork's rule-cited explanation and Manubot's CI checks supply the remaining card fields.

### Cited Findings
- Google eng-practices, "Small CLs": the right size is "one self-contained change" addressing one thing, with related tests and the context a reviewer needs. About 100 lines is usually reasonable and 1,000 usually too large. "When in doubt, write CLs that are smaller than you think you need to write." Reviewers may reject a change "for the sole reason of it being too large." — [Google eng-practices: Small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)
- Same page: put refactorings in a separate CL from feature changes or bug fixes, because each is then easier to understand. Minor cleanups can stay in the main CL. Large changes from a trusted automatic refactoring tool are an exception to the size rule. Stack CLs, sending one and building the next on it, to avoid being blocked. — [Google eng-practices: Small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)
- Sadowski et al., "Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google" (ICSE-SEIP 2018; 12 interviews, 44 survey respondents, logs of about 9M reviewed changes). — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/). Secondary summaries report a median change size of about 24 lines, about 35% of changes touching one file and about 90% touching fewer than 10 files, and a median of one approver. — [U Waterloo lecture slides](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~migod/846/current/summaries/10-Youssef-GoogleCodeReview-slides.pdf). Median wait for first feedback is reportedly under 1 hour for small changes vs about 5 hours for very large ones. — [Graphite blog](https://graphite.com/blog/how-google-does-code-review). These figures are secondary and should be verified against the paper (DOI 10.1145/3183519.3183525).
- SmartBear/Cisco (MeetingPlace team, about 10 months, 2,500 reviews, 50 programmers, 3.2M LOC; 2006, vendor study, report gated): review fewer than 200–400 LOC at a time, inspect at under 300–500 LOC/hour, and keep sessions to 60–90 minutes or less. — [StickyMinds summary](https://www.stickyminds.com/article/largest-case-study-code-reviews-ever); [SmartBear white paper mirror](https://files.cnblogs.com/dangjian/BestPracticesForPeerCodeReview.pdf). Skepticism about generalizing the result is on record. — [OpenStack-dev thread, 2013](https://lists.openstack.org/pipermail/openstack-dev/2013-August/014208.html)
- "The Value of Effective Pull Request Description" (Pirouzkhah, Wurzel Gonçalves, Bacchelli; MSR 2026; arXiv 2602.14611, Feb 2026): an eight-element taxonomy of descriptions, about 80K GitHub PRs from 156 projects, and 64 surveyed developers. **Stating the type of feedback wanted is the element most associated with acceptance and reviewer engagement.** Purpose and code explanations are valued mainly for preserving rationale and history. — [arXiv 2602.14611](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.14611); [MSR 2026 program](https://2026.msrconf.org/details/msr-2026-technical-papers/35/The-Value-of-Effective-Pull-Request-Description)
- Stacked diffs: there is little hard evidence that stacking speeds review. A vendor-circulated claim that 200–400-line PRs had "40% fewer defects" and were "approved three times faster" (1.5M PRs) could not be verified. GitHub previewed native stacked PRs in April 2026. Practitioners suggest a limit of 3–4 PRs per stack. — [InfoQ, Apr 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/04/github-stacked-prs); [contextstudios comparison (unverified stat)](https://www.contextstudios.ai/comparisons/stacked-pull-requests-vs-one-large-pull-request); [Pragmatic Engineer on Meta stacked diffs](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/stacked-diffs)
- InkSync's edit record schema: original text, replacement text, source component, and an adds-new-information flag. — [arXiv 2309.15337](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.15337v1)
- Patchwork's style-guide bot explains each change by citing the guide. — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Manubot CI checks every PR for build breakage and invalid citations before human approval. — [greenelab/meta-review](https://github.com/greenelab/meta-review)

### Inferences
These are concrete designs for this paper, synthesized from the findings above. They are not themselves evidence.

- **Decision card (one per review unit, one screen).**
  1. `ID` + `layer` (beat / bullet / prose) + `location` (section, `\label`, sentence line number), sorted in document order.
  2. `Decision asked`: one of `accept/reject`, `pick A or B`, `needs your judgment: <question>`. This follows the MSR 2026 finding on stating the feedback type.
  3. `Class` + risk flag: `wording` | `structure/move` | `claim/hedge` | `number` | `citation`. Anything other than `wording` gets the InkSync-style ⚠ "changes what the paper asserts".
  4. `Intent`: one line, with rule or comment cited (e.g. "Overleaf comment #c17" or "style rule S4: no hedging stacks").
  5. `Diff`: word-level before/after, at most about 3 sentences. Anything larger is split.
  6. `Evidence`: for number or claim cards, the macro name(s) → value → source CSV/script + commit hash, and the old vs new value. A sentence about evidence is not enough.
  7. `Checks passed`: compiles; all `\macros` resolve; no bare numerals introduced outside macros; cite keys exist; no orphaned `\ref`; latexdiff renders. A failed check means the card is not served.
  8. `Default`: what happens if the reviewer ignores the card (e.g. wording cards auto-accept after the next pass; claim cards never auto-accept).
- **Sizing.** By analogy to Google's "one self-contained change", one card is one rhetorical move, such as one claim reworded or one paragraph reordered. Mechanical sweeps (sembr reflow, macro renames, typo batches) follow the "trusted automatic refactoring" exception. They go in one bulk card with a count and a sample, separate from substantive cards, mirroring the rule to separate refactors from features.
- **Stacking.** Each card is one commit on a review branch, ordered top-down (beats → bullets → prose). Rejecting a beat card invalidates its dependent bullet and prose cards, which the agent then regenerates. This is the paper analogue of stacked diffs.
- **Session budget.** By analogy to the Cisco and InkSync numbers, keep a sitting to a bounded deck (e.g. tens of cards, not hundreds). Put ⚠ cards first while attention is fresh, and give them about 30–60 s each (InkSync verification averaged 44 s).

### Gaps
- No empirical study of "decision cards" or change briefs for prose edits was found. The card design above transfers code-review evidence by analogy.
- The primary Sadowski 2018 numbers were not verified from the paper text, and the SmartBear report's primary data is gated.
- No controlled evidence was found that stacked diffs reduce review time.

## Q4. Should review be layered (approve beats first, then bullets, then prose)? Is there evidence that top-down review saves time?

### Takeaway
There is no controlled study of top-down layered review for documents. The best available support is (a) Google's code-review navigation guidance and its rationale, which is to check the broad direction first and send design objections immediately because they make detailed review wasted effort, and (b) the CHI-adjacent finding that AI help is most valuable and least risky at local edits, while global restructuring is where chat and AI help showed no measured advantage. Layering is well motivated as *waste avoidance*, not as a measured time saving.

### Cited Findings
- Google eng-practices, "Navigating a CL in review", gives three steps. (1) Take a broad view: does the change make sense at all? If not, "respond immediately with an explanation of why the change should not be happening." (2) Examine the main parts first. If there are major design problems, "send those comments immediately", even before reviewing the rest. (3) Review the rest in a logical sequence. — [Google eng-practices: Navigating a CL](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/navigate.html)
- Rationale given on the same page: major design problems make the rest of the review less useful because much of the code may be removed or rewritten. Developers often build follow-up work during review, and large redesigns take longer, so early high-level feedback prevents wasted effort. — [Google eng-practices: Navigating a CL](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/navigate.html)
- If a CL is too large to tell which parts matter, the reviewer should ask where to start or ask for it to be split. — [Google eng-practices: Navigating a CL](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/navigate.html)
- AnchoredAI: anchored span-level AI help changed behaviour on local tasks but showed no significant difference in the share of the document changed on global (organization/argument) tasks. The authors recommend aligning modality to task: anchoring for local edits, chat for broad restructuring. — [arXiv 2509.16128](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16128v1)
- Patchwork: AI-generated branch names and edit descriptions (the "meta-work") were among the most useful AI contributions, i.e. AI summaries as navigation over edits. — [Litt 2024](https://buttondown.com/geoffreylitt/archive/towards-universal-version-control-with-patchwork)
- Stack-dependency rationale: build the next CL on the one under review so work is not blocked, with each CL leaving the system working. — [Google eng-practices: Small CLs](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/developer/small-cls.html)

### Inferences
- **Mapping to the existing layer system.** The beats layer corresponds to Google step 1 ("should this change exist / is the direction right?"). Bullets correspond to step 2 ("the main parts"), and prose to step 3. A beat rejection should immediately cancel or regenerate the dependent bullet and prose cards, so the user never reviews prose under a beat they would have rejected.
- **Gate rule.** Agents should not generate prose-level cards for a section until its beat card and bullet cards are accepted. The prose layer then carries only `wording`-class cards plus any ⚠ cards where prose drifted from an accepted bullet (detectable by an agent check: "does this sentence still say what bullet B3 says?").
- **What each layer serves.** Beats are a one-line-per-section diff against the last accepted beats, so the reviewer sees only changed beats. Bullets are card decks for changed bullets only. Prose is a word-diff and latexdiff PDF of changed sentences only, plus the bulk mechanical card. Unchanged material is never re-served, which keeps attention on deltas.
- **Expected savings mechanism.** The time saved comes from avoided rework: prose never written or reviewed under a rejected beat. The Google rationale supports this, but no measured effect size exists for documents.

### Gaps
- No experimental or field evidence was found quantifying time saved by top-down (outline-first) review of documents vs linear review. Literature on reverse outlining or hierarchical document review was not searched within the tool budget.
- No evidence was found on whether approving an outline biases reviewers to under-scrutinize later prose (a possible automation-bias side effect of layering).
