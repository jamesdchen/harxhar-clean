# General style guides and scientific-writing research, compared with the paper-prose skill

Scope: the classic and evidence-based general writing guides (Gopen & Swan; Williams & Bizup; Pinker; Strunk & White and
Pullum's critique), the empirical literature on reading and on academic registers, and publisher and journal guides,
each mapped against `.claude/skills/paper-prose/SKILL.md`, `writeup/style/voice.md` and the 18 pattern files under
`writeup/style/patterns/` (V1–V16, AP-V, AP-S). Report §4.3 (cadence, voice, stance, Rule NT), §4.5 (Cochrane, Head) and
§7.2 (JFEc limits) are not repeated here; they are cited by section where they bear on a point.

Evidence grades follow the report's scheme, with a sub-label for the kind of evidence: **D-exp** (a reading,
comprehension or rating experiment), **D-corpus** (a corpus count), **D-obs** (an observational correlation, for example
readability against citations), **D-mandated** (a journal requirement), **P** (prescriptive: a guide's opinion with no
measured effect), **I** (my inference). **[memory]** marks a claim from my own knowledge that I did not re-verify in this
session; every such claim sits under Gaps or is labelled where it appears.

## 1. Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (1990): topic position, stress position, old before new

### Takeaway
Gopen & Swan give seven positional "reader expectation" principles: the subject followed soon by its verb, new
information in the stress position at the sentence's end, old linking information and the story's "character" in the
topic position at its start, the action in the verb, and context before anything new. They say outright that these are
not rules, and they cite no experiments. The given-new experiments of Haviland & Clark supply measured support for
old-before-new. The skill has nothing on information order, and V1's literal wording ("scope, conditions and tests after
them") pushes old context into the stress position.

### Cited Findings
- Citation: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist* 78(6):550–558,
  Nov–Dec 1990 — [AcaWiki](https://acawiki.org/The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing); original online at
  [American Scientist](https://www.americanscientist.org/blog/the-long-view/the-science-of-scientific-writing) (returned
  HTTP 503 in this session; the text was read from a verbatim copy at
  [TU/e mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html)).
- The seven structural principles, verbatim from the article's closing list — [TU/e mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html):
  1. "Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb."
  2. "Place in the stress position the 'new information' you want the reader to emphasize."
  3. "Place the person or thing whose 'story' a sentence is telling at the beginning of the sentence, in the topic position."
  4. "Place appropriate 'old information' (material already stated in the discourse) in the topic position for linkage backward and contextualization forward."
  5. "Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb."
  6. "In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new."
  7. "In general, try to ensure that the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the relative expectations for emphasis raised by the structure."
- Stress position: "It is a linguistic commonplace that readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end
  of a sentence. We refer to that location as a 'stress position.'" Topic position: "in the topic position the reader
  needs and expects perspective and context"; "Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up
  first." — [TU/e mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html)
- One function per unit: "Each unit of discourse, no matter what the size, is expected to serve a single function, to
  make a single point." — [TU/e mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html)
- Not rules: "None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'"; "There can be no fixed algorithm
  for good writing"; "any reader expectation can be violated to good effect". The article cites no experiments; it claims
  the principles give the writer "far greater control (although never complete control)" of interpretation. (Grade: P.)
  — [TU/e mirror](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html)
- No direct experimental test of the topic and stress positions as Gopen frames them turned up. A secondary page states
  that the approach's "strongest empirical claim is that violations of these expectations measurably slow reader
  comprehension at the local scale", but it names no primary study, so the claim is unverified —
  [Reader Expectation Approach notes](https://seandavi.github.io/scriptorium/concepts/knowledge/scientific-writing/reader-expectation-approach/).
- Measured support for old-before-new: Haviland & Clark, "What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in
  comprehension", *JVLVB* 13:512–521 (1974), three reaction-time experiments on sentence pairs testing a "given-new
  strategy"; under the "Given-New Contract" a speaker builds sentences so the listener has one direct antecedent for the
  given information — [Desterro review](https://periodicos.ufsc.br/index.php/desterro/article/download/9027/8363/26935);
  later reviews summarize that "adults are faster to comprehend sentences for given-new structures (Haviland & Clark,
  1974; see also Chen et al., 2012)" — [PMC4266472](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4266472). Clark's bridging
  paper develops the same contract — [Clark 1977 reprint](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_Bridging_%20reprint%201977.pdf).
  (Grade: D-exp, on lab sentence pairs, not on research prose.)
- Pullum explains Strunk & White's bad passive example by information order: "a passive is always a stylistic train
  wreck when the subject refers to something newer and less established in the discourse than the agent", whereas "The
  bill was paid by an anonymous benefactor" sounds natural. So the active/passive choice is an information-order choice —
  [Pullum 2009](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf).

### Inferences
- **The skill has no pattern on information order** (confirmed by reading the 18 pattern files; the four scope files V13–V16 were read in part). The nearest is V1's
  "Subject and main verb early; scope, conditions and tests after them". It agrees with principle 1 (subject and verb
  early), but its second half conflicts with principles 4 and 6 whenever the scope or condition is old information: it
  moves the old frame into the stress position, where readers put emphasis. (I)
- **V1's own example shows the conflict.** V1 rewrites to "The FOMC calendar carries the gain on statement days. The
  realized moments carry it on ordinary days." If the paragraph is about which predictor group carries the gain, the day
  type is the frame (old) and the group is the news, so G&S would write "On statement days, the FOMC calendar carries the
  gain. On ordinary days, the realized moments carry it." That version is still parallel and plain, it puts the contrast
  frame first and ends each sentence on the new item. V10's rewrite ("On statement days the FOMC calendar lowers the loss
  by …") already uses this order, so the skill is internally inconsistent here. (I)
- **V6 is a special case of the stress-position principle.** A clause-final tail such as ", none significantly" exploits
  the stress position to give an unbacked verdict maximum emphasis. G&S would say the stress position should hold what the
  writer wants emphasized; V6 adds that whatever is emphasized there must carry its number and test. Citing G&S principle
  7 in V6's Motivation would explain why tails "punch". (I)
- **V7's first rewrite breaks principle 1.** "On every bar of the evaluation span, adding the other series to HAR, the
  realized moments and the FOMC calendar does not significantly change the linear models' loss" has a gerund subject of
  about 13 words before its verb, after a 7-word opener: a reader holds about 20 words before learning what happens. A G&S
  rewrite names a short subject first ("The full predictor set does not significantly change the linear models' loss on
  any bar of the evaluation span relative to HAR plus the realized moments and the FOMC calendar ($p \geq$ …)"), or splits
  it. (I)
- **Operational pattern sketch, "V17 Old before new" (proposal; id to be assigned by the catalog owner).**
  - *Intent:* open each sentence with something the reader already has (a name from the notation guide, the previous
    sentence's subject or final noun phrase, a defined set) and end it on the new number or finding.
  - *Applicability:* agent-drafted body sentences, beats and bullets (S0); not author text (V13) or Overleaf text (V15);
    not titles (V9).
  - *Rewrite:* a short old frame ("On statement days,", "For the linear models,", "Relative to HAR,") may precede the
    subject; the test statistic follows the number in parentheses; no citation chain, "respectively" or restated scope at
    sentence end when the finding is the news.
  - *Detection (I, no published detector):* parse each sentence's first noun phrase and test whether its head lemma, or a
    registered name or set from `writeup/style/sets.json` (the registry V5 already plans), appears in the previous one or
    two sentences; flag sentences where the topic noun phrase is new **and** the final noun phrase is old (inverted
    order). This reuses V5 Stage A's machinery. Flags go to review, never to automatic rewrite, since G&S say expectations
    "can be violated to good effect".
  - *Basis:* D-exp for given-before-new (Haviland & Clark) transferred to research prose (T-genre); P for the positional
    rules (G&S; Williams; Pullum's passive analysis).
- **Operational pattern sketch, "V18 Subject close to its verb".** Flag a main clause where more than about 10 words
  separate the subject head from the finite verb, or where an introductory element longer than about 12 words precedes
  the subject. Rewrite: shorten the subject to a named object (V3), move the long material after the verb, or split.
  Detection: dependency parse (spaCy `nsubj` → `ROOT` token distance). The thresholds are my guesses (I) and should be
  calibrated on corpus papers once the corpus exists (§4.4). Basis: P (G&S principle 1; Strunk & White "Keep related
  words together"; Williams [memory]).
- **Old-before-new governs voice choice better than a voice rule.** The report leaves passive share to corpus bands
  (§4.3.2). G&S and Pullum give a per-sentence criterion: use the passive when it puts old information first ("These
  forecasts are scored by QLIKE" after a sentence about the forecasts), the active when the agent is old. That criterion
  can be checked sentence by sentence before any corpus exists, and it does not push voice share in either direction. (I)

### Gaps
- No primary experiment testing G&S's topic and stress positions on scientific prose was found; the "slows
  comprehension" claim on the secondary page is unverified.
- Haviland & Clark's actual reading-time effect sizes were not retrieved.
- A Michigan State thesis (Maier) on passage thematization surfaced but its results were not read —
  [MSU ETD 45400](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/45400/OBJ/download).
- [memory] Gopen later expanded the approach in *Expectations: Teaching Writing from the Reader's Perspective* and *The
  Sense of Structure* (both 2004), which add paragraph-level positions (the issue at the paragraph's start, the point at
  its end or start). Not checked.
- The detection thresholds in the V17 and V18 sketches are untested.

## 2. Williams & Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (12th ed.): characters, actions, nominalizations, cohesion and coherence, concision, shape

### Takeaway
Williams turns G&S into a revision procedure: characters as subjects, actions as verbs; revise nominalizations that hide
actions; old-to-new for cohesion; consistent topic strings for coherence; emphasis at the end; and a list of concision
moves. The skill covers concision through V5, AP-V and AP-S. It defers nominalizations, has no cohesion or topic-string
rule, and deliberately bans one of Williams's recommended devices, the participial free modifier (AP-S2).

### Cited Findings
- The 12th edition is by Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup; Bizup revised the last editions and ran a Pearson webinar on
  teaching it — [Pearson](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/higher-education/insights-and-events/webinars-events/2015/03/teaching-with-style-using-joseph-williamss-classic-guide-with-students.html);
  [Wikipedia: Joseph M. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_M._Williams).
- Lesson sequence includes separate lessons on Actions, Characters, Cohesion and Coherence, and Global Coherence (publisher
  listing, per search summary) — [Schweitzer listing](https://www.schweitzer-online.de/buch/Williams/Style/9780321953308/A1644480/).
- Characters and actions: subjects should name the characters and verbs their actions; "A sentence is a story about
  characters doing things." Nominalizations are "Nouns that are created from verbs or adjectives"; when a sentence has
  too many, turn them back into verbs and adjectives — [Buteau summary of Williams & Bizup](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/);
  the Characters lesson also covers turning abstractions into characters — [blog on the Characters lesson](https://kspeicherwrite.blogspot.com/2006/03/in-characters-fourth-lesson-of-style.html). (Grade: P.)
- Cohesion: "The 'old-to-new' principle is the most important principle of cohesion." Coherence: keep "a consistent
  string of topic words throughout a paragraph to keep it focused." Emphasis: "The most emphatic part of a sentence is
  its end." — [Buteau summary](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/). (Grade: P.)
- Concision moves listed: delete meaningless words ("kind of", "really"); delete doubled words and redundant categories
  ("full and complete"); replace a phrase with a word ("due to the fact that" → "because"); "Change negatives to
  affirmatives" ("not different" → "similar") — [Buteau summary](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/).
- Agency: the summary warns that "The passive voice is often used to hide the agent of an action" and advises "Don't be
  afraid of the word 'I.'" — [Buteau summary](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/).
- Measured evidence on nominal versus verbal style:
  - Bostian & Thering, "Scientists: Can They Read What They Write?", *J. Technical Writing and Communication*
    17(4):417–427 (1987), [doi:10.2190/Q4YK-UMCU-D6BW-3AGE](https://doi.org/10.2190/Q4YK-UMCU-D6BW-3AGE) (metadata via
    [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2190/Q4YK-UMCU-D6BW-3AGE)): international scientists (English as first,
    second or third language) comprehended nominal style about as well as active style but read it more slowly; lower-
    proficiency readers had proportionally more trouble; scientists preferred active writing and rated its authors more
    favorably (findings from a search summary of the abstract, not the full text). (Grade: D-exp.)
  - Venagli et al. (2025, workshop abstract): eye-tracking of 40 Italian adults; accuracy at ceiling in all conditions,
    but answering a question about a passage with a full-argument nominalization cost longer fixations and more
    regressions; "real-time processing appears to be facilitated when events are realized as (active or passive) verbal
    structures"; cites Wolfer (2016) on legal text, where unpacked texts were read faster — [Venagli et al. 2025](https://tmalsburg.github.io/ComprehensionWorkshop/abstracts/VenagliEtAl2025). (Grade: D-exp; Italian, small n.)
  - Spyridakis & Isakson, "Nominalizations vs. Denominalizations: Do They Influence What Readers Recall?", *JTWC*
    28:163–188 (1998) exists — [doi:10.2190/01HD-MHU1-QNX9-R3YE](https://doi.org/10.2190/01hd-mhu1-qnx9-r3ye) — but its
    results were not retrieved (see Gaps).
- The LLM side (already in the report, §4.2 and §4.3.1, not repeated): GPT-4o used nominalizations at about 2.1 times the
  human rate and agentless passives at about half — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2).

### Inferences
- **Characters-as-subjects is partly in the skill.** V1 (subject and verb early) and V10 (no inanimate subject with an
  evaluative verb) cover the worst case. No pattern asks who the sentence's character is. For this paper the natural
  characters are the forecasts, models, predictor groups and "we" (V3 names), and the natural actions are lower, raise,
  carry, beat, remove. (I)
- **A narrow nominalization rule is cheap and well supported.** Williams's target is the nominalization that hides the
  sentence's action behind an empty verb. The reading-time evidence (Bostian & Thering; Venagli; Wolfer) favours verbal
  forms on speed with no comprehension loss from unpacking. LLM drafts over-use nominalizations (Reinhart, 2.1×). The
  corpus evidence (Section 5, Biber & Gray) warns against wholesale denominalization of a nominal register. A rule
  limited to light-verb constructions satisfies all three: flag "perform/conduct/carry out an estimation/analysis/
  comparison of", "provide/yield/lead to an improvement/reduction/increase in", "make a comparison", "there is a
  reduction in", "achieve a reduction". Rewrite: the verb ("estimate", "improves", "lowers"). Keep nominalizations that
  name a defined object (QLIKE loss, the evaluation span, the forecast combination) or that sum up the previous sentence
  (a "summative" nominal that serves old-before-new). Detection: a regex over a light-verb list plus a -tion/-ment/
  -ance/-ence/-ity suffix list, flags to review. This would be the first drafting rule in the area AP-S marks
  "Measured or catalogued, with no rule". (I; basis D-exp + D-corpus + P.)
- **Topic strings are the paragraph-level counterpart of V3.** V3 makes one object carry one name; a topic-string check
  makes the subjects of a paragraph's sentences name a few recurring characters. Detection: list the main-clause subject
  heads per paragraph and flag paragraphs in which no subject head recurs and none matches the paragraph's first
  sentence. (I; basis P plus Hillier's D-obs finding in Section 5 that sentence-to-sentence connectivity tracks
  citations.)
- **Concision is mostly covered, and better.** Williams's "delete what readers can infer" and "delete meaningless words"
  are V5's NEEDED? test and its REDUNDANT-SCOPE and BOOSTER codes; "replace a phrase with a word" is AP-V3 ("use", not
  "utilize"); metadiscourse is AP-S5. The skill adds what Williams lacks: a per-instance test and logged reason codes.
- **"Change negatives to affirmatives" must not be adopted for statistical claims.** "Does not significantly change" is
  not equivalent to "leaves unchanged", and V16 rightly protects negations. If the move is borrowed, it should be limited
  to lexical negatives with no test behind them ("not different" → "similar" only when no test is reported). (I)
- **Free modifiers: a deliberate departure.** Williams teaches resumptive, summative and free modifiers for extending a
  sentence without sprawl [memory]. AP-S2 bans participial tails because LLMs produce them at about 5.3 times the human
  rate (D). Keep AP-S2, and note in its file that it departs from Williams for a measured reason. A summative modifier
  (", a gain that …") is not a participial tail and is not caught by AP-S2's grep; it can be V6-like when it carries a
  verdict. (I)

### Gaps
- No primary text of the 12th edition was available; quotations come from a practitioner summary (Buteau) that names the
  12th edition as its main source but gives no page numbers.
- [memory] Williams's lesson list in recent editions: Understanding Style; Correctness; Actions; Characters; Cohesion and
  Coherence; Emphasis; Motivation; Global Coherence; Concision; Shape; Elegance; Ethics. Not verified against the 12th
  edition.
- [memory] Williams's diagnostics: underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence and check that they name a
  character and reach the verb quickly; circle nominalizations; list the topic strings of a paragraph. His concision
  lesson includes "delete metadiscourse" and a section on hedges and intensifiers. His Global Coherence lesson puts a
  document's point at the end of its introduction and a paragraph's point at the end of its "issue" (its first sentence
  or two). Not verified.
- [memory] Williams lists when nominalizations are acceptable: as a short subject referring to a previous sentence ("These
  arguments …"), naming what would otherwise be "the fact that", as the object of a verb, or as a common concept term.
  Not verified; this is the carve-out the proposed rule above needs.
- Spyridakis & Isakson (1998) results were not retrieved; [memory] my recollection is that the effects on recall were
  mixed, which is why I do not rely on it.

## 3. Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014): classic style, curse of knowledge, metadiscourse, hedging, the tree, coherence, critique of Strunk & White

### Takeaway
Pinker supplies the reasons behind several of our rules (hedging "a choice, not a tic"; metadiscourse helps the writer,
not the reader; zombie nouns and "metaconcepts") and one rule we lack, the curse-of-knowledge check. His claims rest on
linguistics and cognitive psychology, not on tests of the advice; a *Nature* reviewer noted that he does not name the
research behind his defence of the passive. His view of hedging matches V7 almost word for word.

### Cited Findings
- Classic style: "The writer can see something that the reader has not yet noticed", and orients the reader to see it;
  prose as a window onto a truth the writer already knows — [Pinker, Chronicle, 26 Sep 2014, reprinted](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/).
  (Grade: P.)
- Metadiscourse is "verbiage about verbiage" and "is there to help the writer, not the reader"; he recommends signposts
  sparingly — [Pinker 2014](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/). A Harvard
  computer scientist disagrees in part: much of the metadiscourse Pinker attacks is unneeded, but "appropriate advance
  signposting can ease the job of the reader considerably" — [Shieber, Harvard blog, 2014](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/pamphlet/2014/09/29/inaccessible-writing-in-both-senses-of-the-term/).
- Hedging: appropriate when it qualifies ("on average", "all things being equal"); "It's not that good writers never hedge
  their claims. It's that their hedging is a choice, not a tic." He advises stating an effect's size and the claim's
  certainty directly — [Pinker 2014](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/). In a
  2012 talk he called it "compulsive hedging", signalled by "somewhat, comparatively, and to a certain degree" —
  [Harvard Gazette 2012](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge).
- Nominalization "takes a perfectly spry verb and embalms it into a lifeless noun" (Helen Sword's "zombie nouns");
  metaconcepts are "concepts about concepts" such as "issue", "level", "context", "strategy" —
  [Pinker 2014](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/).
- Curse of knowledge: "a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know",
  "a major reason that good scholars write bad prose"; experts "chunk" knowledge and forget readers lack it; he advises
  showing drafts to real readers — [Pinker 2014](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/);
  "extraordinarily difficult to know what it's like not to know" — [Harvard Gazette 2015](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/bringing-sanity-to-clarity).
- Evidence base: the Chronicle essay cites Helen Sword's style analysis of 500 scholarly articles (a healthy minority in
  every field written with grace) and lab demonstrations of the curse of knowledge, but no study of readers of academic
  prose — [Pinker 2014](https://davidlabaree.com/2021/07/08/pinker-why-academics-stink-at-writing/). The 2015 Gazette
  interview cites no studies — [Harvard Gazette 2015](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/bringing-sanity-to-clarity).
- Passive: Pinker defends the "unfairly maligned passive voice" and calls Strunk & White "an early and amusing example"
  of the style-guide genre — [Harvard Gazette 2015](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/03/bringing-sanity-to-clarity).
  He says research shows the passive lets a writer direct the reader's gaze like a camera angle; Paul Raeburn's *Nature*
  review notes that he does not say which research (per search summary) — [Nature 513:309](https://www.nature.com/articles/513309a).
  Michael Leddy argues that Pinker misstates Strunk & White, who explicitly do not ban the passive —
  [Leddy 2013](https://mleddy.blogspot.com/2013/06/pinker-on-strunk-and-white.html);
  [Leddy 2018](https://mleddy.blogspot.com/2018/01/oliver-kamm-on-elements-of-style.html).
- Book structure: ch. 3 "The Curse of Knowledge", ch. 4 "The Web, the Tree, and the String", ch. 5 "Arcs of
  Coherence" — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/the-sense-of-style/chapters-5-6-summary). A reviewer
  paraphrases ch. 5: placing new information in an evoked context is why "old, then new", "given, then inferred", "cause
  and effect" and "summary, then elaboration" orders work — [Geoff Hart review](https://geoff-hart.com/articles/2016/sense-of-style.html).

### Inferences
- **Hedging: covered.** V7 (hedge mechanism and interpretation, state computed facts plainly, scope to sample, period and
  test) is Pinker's "choice, not a tic" made checkable. Pinker's acceptable hedges ("on average", "all things being
  equal") are scope qualifiers that V7 requires. (I)
- **Compulsive hedging is a human failure; LLM drafts fail the other way.** The report documents LLM over-certainty and
  scope inflation (§4.3.6, Zhou et al., Peters & Chin-Yee). The skill rightly polices both directions; a pure "cut
  hedges" rule borrowed from Pinker would be wrong for agent text. (I)
- **Metadiscourse: covered for importance-assertions, ambiguous for structure.** AP-S5 targets "it is worth noting",
  "It should be noted that", "In other words". It does not say whether a structural preview ("Section 4 reports the
  ablations") is allowed. Pinker would cut most; Shieber and the signalling experiments in Section 5 (Lorch) support
  previews for texts with complex topic structure; Head's introduction formula (report §4.5) includes a roadmap. AP-S5
  should state that a roadmap of sections and an endophoric pointer to an exhibit are not signposting in its sense. (I)
- **Metaconcepts: missing and cheap.** A density flag (AP-V2-style, a rate rather than a ban) on "issue", "level",
  "context", "framework", "approach", "aspect", "dimension", "role", "nature", "perspective", "strategy" would catch
  Pinker's category. Basis: P; I could not find a measurement of metaconcept rates in LLM text. (I)
- **Curse of knowledge: partly covered outside the skill.** The notation guide's §6 requires each symbol to be defined at
  first use and bans code identifiers in body text; V3 bans synonyms chosen for variety. The skill's checklist does not
  include the first-use definition check. Adding a line "every symbol, acronym and named set is defined before or at
  first use (notation guide §6)" is cheap. The jargon experiments (Section 5) show definitions help less than plain
  terms, so the check should prefer a plain term to a defined coinage where one exists. (I)
- **The "tree": partly covered.** Pinker's syntax chapter argues against heavy left branches and garden paths [memory];
  this is the V18 sketch in Section 1. (I)

### Gaps
- The book's own text was not available; Pinker's treatment of given/new, end-weight and the passive in chs. 4–5 is known
  here only through reviews. [memory] Pinker presents the passive as a tool for putting given before new and for keeping
  a consistent topic, credits classic style to Thomas & Turner's *Clear and Simple as the Truth* (1994), and warns
  against "elegant variation" (the problem V3 solves). Not verified.
- No experiment testing classic style or the curse-of-knowledge remedy on academic readers was found.

## 4. Strunk & White, *The Elements of Style*: which rules hold up and which are criticized

### Takeaway
Pullum's critique separates the book's style advice ("mostly harmless", often "useless" because unoperational) from its
grammar advice ("atrocious"): three of its four "passive" examples are not passives, and the authors break their own
rules on the same page. The rules that hold up (keep related words together, emphatic words at the end, specific
language) are G&S's positional principles in older words. Our skill already avoids the two rules most criticized: it has
no blanket passive rule, and Rule NT (V5) is an operational version of "omit needless words" that answers Pullum's
objection.

### Cited Findings
- Venue: Geoffrey K. Pullum, "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice", *The Chronicle Review*, issue dated 17 April 2009 —
  [Pullum 2009](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf).
- On the style advice: "mostly harmless. Some of the recommendations are vapid, like 'Be clear' … Many are useless, like
  'Omit needless words.' (The students who know which words are needless don't need the instruction.)" —
  [Pullum 2009](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf).
- On the passive: of "the four pairs of examples offered to show readers what to avoid and how to correct it, a
  staggering three out of the four are mistaken diagnoses"; only "At dawn the crowing of a rooster could be heard" is a
  passive. Writing tutors "simply red-circle everything that looks like a passive", as Word's grammar checker does —
  [Pullum 2009](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf).
- Strunk & White themselves say they do not mean "that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice", which is
  "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary", and give examples where the choice "may depend on the topic under
  discussion" — [Pullum 2009](https://pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/50years.pdf); the same point is made against Pinker and Kamm

- Self-violation: the sentence explaining "Keep related words together" ("The subject of a sentence and the principal verb
  should not, as a rule, be separated by a phrase or clause that can be transferred to the beginning") is "a negative

  violation." "There is of course nothing wrong with writing passives and negatives and adjectives and adverbs." —

- APA 7th edition allows both voices and flags over-use of the passive as the problem (Publication Manual §4.13) —

  per search summary).

### Inferences
- **What holds up, mapped:**
  - "Keep related words together" (subject near verb) → V1 partly; the V18 sketch would make it checkable.
  - "Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end" [memory, S&W rule] → missing; it is G&S's stress position (V17).
  - "Use definite, specific, concrete language" [memory] → covered in substance by V2 (numbers), V4 (verdict words from
    predicates) and V10 (state the number, not an evaluative verb).
  - "Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form" [memory] → missing; a parallelism check for lists and paired claims is
    cheap and fits V1's split sentences (Section 7).
  - "Make the paragraph the unit of composition" and begin with a topic sentence [memory] → partly: the L4 design makes
    each beat's claim the paragraph's topic sentence (report §8.1; V8).
- **What to avoid:**
  - A blanket active-voice rule. The notation guide's §6 ("'We' and active voice") is such a rule. The report already
    flags it as a likely mismatch with data and methods norms (§4.3.2), and for LLM text it pushes in the direction
    models already drift (Reinhart: agentless passives at about half the human rate). The skill correctly sets no
    voice rule (V10 Applicability); the notation guide line should be amended or overridden in the skill's text. (I)
  - Mechanical "omit needless words". Pullum's objection is that the rule gives no test. V5's NEEDED? supplies three tests
    (scope contrast, unrecoverable scope, the paper's own claim) and logs a reason code, so it is the operational form.
    Do not extend deletion beyond V5's lexicon to connectives or scope hedges, which carry information (Section 5). (I)
  - "Put statements in positive form" for statistical negatives (V16 protects negations). (I)
  - Grammar-checker passive detection as a style signal: Pullum's misdiagnosed examples are the failure mode; the report's
    PassivePy (about 98% agreement) is the right tool if voice is ever measured (§4.4). (I)

- The text of *The Elements of Style* was not re-read; the rule wordings marked [memory] above are from my knowledge of

## 5. Evidence-based work: comprehension and readability studies, Hartley, plain language, hedging and metadiscourse (Hyland), nominal style (Biber), effects on reviewers and readers

### Takeaway
Experiments support a few sentence-level rules: given before new, verbal rather than nominal statements of events (on
reading speed), plain terms over jargon even when it is defined, short common words over long ones, and structural
signals for complex texts. Corpus work shows that expert academic prose is nominal and phrasally compressed, which limits
how far denominalization should go. Observational studies in economics link readable abstracts and introductions to
citations and to Top-5 publication, but they are confounded, and one old experiment found harder prose rated as more
competent. No study found shows that following a style guide changes referee decisions.

### Cited Findings
- **Nominal, compressed academic register (D-corpus).** Biber & Gray, "Challenging stereotypes about academic writing:
  Complexity, elaboration, explicitness", *J. English for Academic Purposes* 9(1):2–20 (2010): subordinate clauses are far
  more frequent in conversation; academic writing relies on phrasal modifiers inside noun phrases and is "compressed"
  and less explicit; such styles "are efficient for expert readers, who can quickly extract large amounts of information
  from relatively short, condensed texts. However, they pose difficulties for novice readers, who must learn to infer
  unspecified meaning relations among grammatical constituents." —
  [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/challenging-stereotypes-about-academic-writing-complexity-elabora/)
  (abstract excerpt via fetch and search summary); the 2016 book develops the "loss of explicitness" —
  [Cambridge, *Grammatical Complexity in Academic English*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/grammatical-complexity-in-academic-english/loss-of-explicitness-in-academic-research-writing/54AB8FAE6EA978DFD68C639536519FD0).
- **Coherence and reader knowledge (D-exp).** McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch, "Are Good Texts Always Better?",
  *Cognition and Instruction* 14(1):1–43 (1996): low-knowledge learners did better with a coherent text; high-knowledge
  learners did better on situation-model measures with a minimally coherent one, through compensatory inference; the
  advantage did not show on surface recall; samples were school students reading science and history texts —
  [BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing);
  [course notes](https://eng574research.blogspot.com/2011/02/eng-574-are-good-texts-always-better.html) (both secondary).
- **Signalling devices (D-exp).** Lorch's review (*Educational Psychology Review*, 1989): signals highlight structure
  without adding content and improve memory for the cued information —
  [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01320135); in Lorch & Lorch, signalling had no effect on recall when
  topic structure was simple and changed recall organisation when it was complex —
  [UKY record](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/);
  headings and preview sentences both improved outlining —
  [UKY record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/signaling-topic-structure-via-headings-or-preview-sentences/).
  (College-student samples; details per search summary.)
- **Nominal vs. verbal style (D-exp):** Bostian & Thering (1987) and Venagli et al. (2025), Section 2.
- **Jargon (D-exp).** Bullock, Colón Amill, Shulman & Dixon, *Public Understanding of Science* 28(7):845–853 (2019),
  N = 650: jargon lowered processing fluency even when definitions were provided, with more resistance to persuasion —
  [PubMed 31354058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31354058/); Shulman et al., *J. Language and Social Psychology*
  (2020): jargon disrupts fluent processing even with definitions and lowers scientific engagement —
  [SAGE figshare](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/The_Effects_of_Jargon_on_Processing_Fluency_Self-Perceptions_and_Scientific_Engagement/4836486).
  Lay readers, not referees.
- **Needlessly long words (D-exp).** Oppenheimer, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20:139–156 (2006): replacing words with
  their longest synonyms lowered readers' ratings of the author's intelligence; "the use of overly complex words leads to
  lower evaluations of a text's author" —
  [Daily Princetonian](https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2006/10/on-erudite-vernacular-i-e-big-words);
  [Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2010/02/19/eloquence-or-elitism-the-concept-of-sounding-smart/) (secondary;
  accounts of the materials differ).
- **The counter-result (D-exp, small n).** Armstrong, "Unintelligible Management Research and Academic Prestige",
  *Interfaces* 10(2):80–86 (1980): journal prestige correlated +0.7 with fog index across 10 management journals; 32
  faculty rated harder-to-read versions of the same passages as showing more research competence —
  [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:orinte:v:10:y:1980:i:2:p:80-86);
  [PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1980-armstrong.pdf). The measure is Flesch-based and crude; a 1989
  follow-up suggested dropping the unreliable economics-journal result (per search summary) —
  [Armstrong working paper](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/get/papers/0502/0502053.pdf).
- **Hartley on abstracts (D-exp, with caveats).** Hartley & Benjamin (1998), British Psychological Society journals:
  structured abstracts were "significantly more readable, significantly longer, and significantly more informative" and
  judges assessed them "more quickly and with significantly less difficulty"; but many comparisons used abstracts
  rewritten into structured form, and abstracts originally published in each format showed no significant readability
  difference (Hartley & Sydes); Hartley notes "considerable debate" about readability formulas' validity — 
  [Hartley, cogprints 587](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/587/1/199801001.html) (search summary;
  the page returned 403 to a direct fetch).
- Hartley, Sotto & Pennebaker, "Style and substance in psychology: are influential articles more readable than less
  influential ones?", *Social Studies of Science* 32(2):321–334 (2002): four studies; results not retrieved —
  [Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/31bab0146984f60cd3358b06e3f873a9).
- **Readability trend (D-corpus).** Plavén-Sigray et al., *eLife* 6:e27725 (2017): 709,577 abstracts from 123 journals,
  1881–2015; Flesch Reading Ease and New Dale–Chall show declining readability, linked to rising general scientific
  jargon — [PMC5584989](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584989).
- **Readability and outcomes in economics (D-obs).**
  - Dowling, Hammami & Zreik, "Easy to read, easy to cite?", *Economics Letters* 173:100–103 (2018): readability of
    *Economics Letters* abstracts "is positively related to future citations", particularly for mathematical and
    quantitative methods and macroeconomics papers — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecolet/v173y2018icp100-103.html).
  - McCannon, "Readability and research impact", *Economics Letters* 180:76–79 (2019): AER introductions 2000–2009; a
    statistically significant relation between readability and citations; the fetched abstract reads "The hardest to read
    papers suffer a reduction in citations by up to 15 of a standard deviation" (the decimal point appears lost; probably

  - A 2019 *Scientometrics* study of NBER working-paper abstracts reports an effect of readability on the probability of
    publishing in a Top 5 journal and argues earlier citation estimates are biased downward (search summary; title not
    retrieved) — [Scientometrics s11192-019-03332-4](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11192-019-03332-4).
  - Hengel, "Publishing while female", *Economic Journal* 132(648):2951–2991 (2022): female-authored papers are 1–6%
    better written on readability scores (working-paper summary), and the standard readability set is Flesch Reading
    Ease, Flesch–Kincaid, Gunning Fog and SMOG; a secondary summary says papers by women took about six months longer in
    review — [UCL PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10148349/1/publishing_female.pdf);
    [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cam:camdae:1753).
  - Hillier, Kelly & Klinger, "Narrative Style Influences Citation Frequency in Climate Change Science", *PLOS ONE*
    (2016), 732 abstracts: of six narrative indicators, conjunctions (Spearman ρ = 0.211) and sentence-to-sentence
    connectivity (ρ = 0.171) were the strongest correlates of citations, then sensory language and appeal; setting and
    narrative perspective ("we") were not significant; narrativity explained about 5% of variance against 37% for journal
    impact factor, and the two were strongly correlated (R² = 0.62), so the effect cannot be separated from journal
    identity — [PMC5158318](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5158318).
- **Plain language (secondary).** Kimble's *Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please* collects about 50 (1st ed.) to 60
  (2nd ed.) studies, 18 on legal documents, reporting that readers including judges and lawyers understand plain language
  better and find it faster to use; the sources found are reviews and Kimble's own columns —
  [Michigan Bar Journal](https://www.michbar.org/journal/Details/ArticleID=4968);
  [Carolina Academic Press](https://cap-press.com/books/isbn/9781531024543/Writing-for-Dollars-Writing-to-Please-Second-Edition).
- **Hedging and metadiscourse (D-corpus).** Hyland, "Writing without conviction? Hedging in science research articles",
  *Applied Linguistics* 17(4):433–454 (1996), 26 molecular biology articles; hedging cannot be understood apart from its
  social and institutional context — [HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/130177);
  [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/display/37954361). Hyland, "Stance and engagement: a model of interaction in academic
  discourse", *Discourse Studies* 7(2):173–192 (2005): 240 articles, eight disciplines, plus informant interviews; stance =
  hedges, boosters, attitude markers, self-mention; engagement = reader-directed features —
  [UEA eprints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/65827/). Studies applying the model report hedges as the most
  frequent interactional marker in research articles —
  [Ensani: political science and religious studies RAs](https://www.ensani.ir/fa/article/573405/interactional-metadiscourse-markers-in-the-discussion-and-conclusion-sections-of-the-research-papers-in-the-disciplines-of-political-sciences-and-religious-studies).
- The report already covers the RCT on promotional language (null effect on perceived merit) and the mixed hedging-trust
  experiments (§4.3.7), and states that "No study measures whether any of this changes referee or editor ratings of

### Inferences
- **Which classic rules have evidence behind them, by grade:**
  - Old before new: D-exp (Haviland & Clark), lab sentences.
  - Verbs over nominalizations for events: D-exp on reading speed (Bostian & Thering; Venagli; Wolfer); comprehension
    accuracy unchanged. Limited by D-corpus (Biber & Gray): expert readers handle compressed prose, and it is the
    register.
  - Plain words over jargon and long words: D-exp (Bullock; Shulman; Oppenheimer) on lay or student readers; T-genre for
    referees.
  - Signposting of structure: D-exp (Lorch) when topic structure is complex; null when simple.
  - Explicit connectives between sentences: D-obs only (Hillier, confounded with journal).
  - Readability overall: D-obs in economics (Dowling; McCannon; the NBER study; Hengel), all formula-based, all
    confounded; one D-exp counter-result (Armstrong) with a small sample and a crude measure.
  - Topic and stress positions, topic strings, characters as subjects, classic style, the curse-of-knowledge remedy,
    parallelism: P.
- **Readability formulas should not be targets.** They measure word and sentence length; Hartley questions their
  validity; Armstrong's result shows referee-like readers can reward difficulty; and V12 already bans global style
  targets. Use them, if at all, as a dashboard beside the corpus bands, never as a gate. (I)
- **Reverse cohesion is a caution, not a licence.** McNamara et al. found that expert readers learn more from less
  explicit text on deep measures; JFEc referees are experts. This argues against over-explicit cohesion (repeated
  summaries, heavy metadiscourse) and is consistent with AP-S5/AP-S6. It does not argue for removing connectives or
  backward links, which cost little and which Hillier's correlation favours. (I)
- **Hyland's model is a ready-made audit frame for the skill's coverage of metadiscourse.** Interactional features:
  hedges (V7), boosters (V5), attitude markers (AP-V3 "no adjectives on one's own results"; V10), self-mention (deferred
  to corpus bands, §4.3.2), engagement markers (no rule; rare in econometrics [memory]). Interactive features:
  transitions (no rule), frame markers (AP-S6 summary openers), endophoric markers ("Table 2 reports", allowed by V10),
  evidentials (citation rules, report §6), code glosses ("In other words" in AP-S5; "that is" and "i.e." unaddressed).
  The gaps are transitions and code glosses. (I)
- **For LLM drafts the classic rules split in value.** "Avoid nominalizations" corrects a measured LLM excess (2.1×).
  "Use the active voice" pushes further in the direction LLMs already drift (agentless passives at half the human rate).
  "Omit needless words" matches the documented verbosity pressure (§4.3.7). So borrow the first and third, not the
  second. (I)

## 6. Publisher and journal guides: JFEc, OUP, Journal of Econometrics, Chicago and APA

### Takeaway
The journal guides say almost nothing about prose style. JFEc fixes an abstract of at most 100 words, 2–6 keywords, JEL
codes, about 40 double-spaced pages, Chicago 15th edition with author-date citations, and end-of-text footnotes, and
offers optional paid language editing; it gives no sentence-level guidance. The Journal of Econometrics guide could not
be fetched. Style for these journals is set by convention, which is why the report calibrates register to a corpus
(§4.4) rather than to a publisher's rules.

**What the general guides do better than the skill**
- They give a mechanism that explains several of our rules at once. G&S's positions explain why V6 tails punch (stress
  position), why V1 splits help (one function per unit), and why Pullum's bad passive is bad (new before old). The skill
  states its rules separately, with LLM-frequency evidence, and has no account of sentence information structure. (I)
- They work at the paragraph level. The skill's units are the sentence, the beat and the section; topic strings, the
  paragraph's point and backward links have no pattern. (I)
- Williams gives a diagnostic procedure a reviewer can run (first words of each sentence, topic strings, nominalizations
  circled) [memory], which fits the skill's Detection sections. (I)

**What the skill does better**
- Evidence grading on every rule; LLM-specific markers with measured rates; numbers and verdict words tied to generated
  facts; Rule NT as a testable "omit needless words"; scope and hedge calibration in both directions; content-preservation
  checks on rewrites (V16); protection of the author's text (V13–V15). None of the general guides addresses an AI drafter
  or a generated-number pipeline. (I)

**What to avoid**
- A blanket passive ban or an active-voice target (row 17). For LLM text it worsens a measured deviation.
- Mechanical concision beyond V5's lexicon: deleting connectives, backward links or scope hedges removes information that
  the evidence suggests readers use (rows 10, 21; Section 5).
- Wholesale denominalization or a nominalization-rate target before corpus bands exist (row 7; Biber & Gray).
- Readability-formula targets (row 25).
- "Positive form" rewrites of statistical negatives (row 19).
- Treating G&S's principles as hard rules: their authors say expectations "can be violated to good effect", so the new
  patterns should flag for review, as V6 does, and never rewrite automatically.
