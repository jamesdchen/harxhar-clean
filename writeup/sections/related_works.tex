\paragraph{Realized-volatility modeling.}
The paper's target and backbone descend from the realized-measure
literature: realized variance as a sum of squared intraday returns
\citep{AndersenBollerslev1997,BarndorffNielsenShephard2004}, its
approximate long memory documented by
\citet{AndersenBollerslevDieboldLabys2003}, and the heterogeneous
autoregressive model of \citet{Corsi2009}, whose daily/weekly/monthly
averages we adapt to a base-2 intraday ladder. Refinements of the HAR
backbone are numerous: measurement-error-dependent coefficients
\citep{BollerslevPattonQuaedvlieg2016}, state-dependent time variation
\citep{BekiermanManner2018}, decoupled short- and long-run behavior
\citep{BennedsenLundePakkanen2022}, joint models of returns and realized
measures \citep{HansenHuangShek2012}, and multiple-indicator intraday
structures \citep{EngleGallo2006}. We keep the backbone deliberately
plain --- per-bar least squares on the lag ladder --- because the paper's
questions concern what can be added to it, and its strength makes every
addition honest: on this panel the HAR backbone's coefficients are
$94$--$97\%$ directionally stable over two decades, so a predictor that
survives next to it carries information the ladder does not.

\paragraph{Machine learning for volatility and return prediction.}
Tree ensembles are the empirical workhorse of the recent
machine-learning literature in asset pricing and volatility
\citep{gu2020empirical,christensen2023machine}, with gradient-boosted
implementations \citep{Ke2017,Chen2016} typically the strongest off the
shelf. Interpretability machinery --- functional ANOVA, additive and
pairwise additive models \citep{Lou2013}, and Shapley-based attribution
\citep{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2018consistent} --- is usually used
\emph{post hoc}. Our use is closer to distillation: the tree fits are
treated as a specification search, and the structure they recover
(sparse pairwise products, gated on the session clock) is written back
into the linear design as explicit columns, so the final model runs in
the same exact rolling least-squares path as every other arm. The
product block is therefore a statement about what the trees know, not
merely a score they achieve.

\paragraph{Regularization geometry.}
The linear estimators are textbook families --- ridge
\citep{HoerlKennard1970}, lasso \citep{Tibshirani1996}, elastic net
\citep{ZouHastie2005}, with the least-angle-regression path
\citep{Efron2004} behind our exact online lasso solve --- but the
question they are put to is less standard: not which family predicts
best on average, but what the head-to-head reveals about how predictive
coefficients are distributed across hundreds of weakly informative
columns. The dense-but-weak finding (selection loses; uniform heavy
shrinkage wins; no low-rank rotation concentrates the signal) is a
statement about this panel's coefficient geometry, and it is what
licenses the block-penalty construction. A companion finding is about
tuning rather than families: on short causal validation tails, the price
of a hyperparameter mistake is asymmetric across families --- benign for
ridge, destructive for selectors --- which is why the paper's final
model carries fixed penalties rather than tuned ones.

\paragraph{Retransformation.}
Because models are fit on a transformed scale and scored on the variance
scale, the back-transform is part of the estimator, and its literature
is reviewed where the contract is defined
(Section~\ref{sec:smearing_estimator}). In brief: static scalar
corrections are standard, from the log-normal factor of
\citet{AndersenBollerslevDieboldLabys2003} to \citet{Duan1983}'s
nonparametric smear; their value is contested
\citep{DemetrescuGolosnoyTitova2020,HalbleibVoev2011}; time-varying
second moments are standard inside transform-scale models
\citep{CorsiMittnikPigorschPigorsch2008,BollerslevKretschmerPigorschTauchen2009,CiarretaZarraga2016}
but always estimated jointly within one model's likelihood; and the
conditional variant is old outside finance
\citep{Manning1998,ManningMullahy2001}. The contract used here --- a
causal, model-specific second moment applied uniformly at evaluation
time across heterogeneous model classes --- sits between these poles,
and the adaptive recursion that beats the retrospective
\citet{Duan1983} baseline causally is, to our knowledge, new as an
evaluation-layer convention.

\paragraph{Forecast evaluation.}
QLIKE is the strictly consistent loss for the conditional variance
\citep{Patton2011}; comparisons are paired Diebold--Mariano tests
\citep{DieboldMariano1995} with the small-sample correction of
\citet{HarveyLeybourneNewbold1997}, and model confidence sets where
several arms are compared \citep{HansenLundeNason2011}. Calibration is
read through \citet{MincerZarnowitz1969} regressions.
