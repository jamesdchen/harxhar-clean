\section{A Dense but Weak Signal: Lasso versus Ridge}
\label{sec:dense_weak}

% ridge: alpha=1 on HAR-of-target + alpha=100 on HAR-of-all_features.
% Base text: drafts_2026-07-30/linear_vs_nonlinear.tex (linear half).

Section~\ref{sec:buckets} established that the exogenous panel carries
information beyond the HAR lag structure of \citet{Corsi2009}, and that no
single bucket carries much of it. This section holds the information set fixed
at its widest and varies only the \emph{shrinkage geometry} of the linear
estimator that reads it. The contest is between the two canonical responses to
a wide design: $\ell_1$ selection --- the lasso \citep{Tibshirani1996}, which
bets that the signal is concentrated in a small subset of columns and zeroes
the rest --- and $\ell_2$ shrinkage --- ridge \citep{HoerlKennard1970}, which
bets that the signal is spread across many columns and keeps all of them,
small. The elastic net \citep{ZouHastie2005} interpolates between the two.
Which penalty wins is therefore not a tuning detail; it is a measurement of
how the predictive content is distributed across the design. The verdict here
is one-sided --- selection loses to shrinkage everywhere it is tried --- and
that verdict is the section's diagnosis: the signal is \textbf{dense but
weak}, spread thinly across hundreds of columns of which none is individually
strong. The diagnosis then dictates an estimator, and the section ends with
it: a two-block ridge that shrinks the wide exogenous block a hundred times
harder than the persistence backbone, and selects nothing.

\subsection{The Head-to-Head}
\label{sec:dense_weak_headtohead}

\paragraph{Design.}
The three penalized linear estimators are run against nine exogenous feature
buckets on one shared out-of-sample panel of $n = 218{,}934$ thirty-minute
bars, with identical rows in every cell; nothing varies across the grid except
the estimator and the bucket. The panel is computed as 100 walk-forward chunks
over a rolling 500-trading-day ($24{,}000$-bar) training window, and every arm
is refit \emph{every bar}. Each arm re-selects its own penalty periodically
and causally: every 250 solves, the current training window is split forward
into a fit block, a 25-bar embargo (covering the longest moving average
entering the features), and a 125-bar validation tail, and the configuration
minimizing validation MSE on that tail is adopted. No forecast's
hyperparameters ever see its own future. The lasso and elastic-net arms are
solved as exact every-bar online homotopies rather than warm-started batch
approximations, so the head-to-head is not confounded with optimization error;
the ridge arm runs on an exact rank-one update path.

\paragraph{Sparsity loses on every bucket.}
Table~\ref{tab:lasso_vs_ridge} orders the same way in all nine rows: ridge is
the best penalty on every bucket, and the lasso is never best on any. The
margin runs from $0.00022$ (\texttt{implied\_vol}, \texttt{vol\_demand}) to
$0.00119$ on the full design (\texttt{all\_features}: ridge $0.12788$ versus
lasso $0.12907$), and the ordering ridge $<$ elastic net $<$ lasso holds in
eight of the nine rows, the one exception a $2 \times 10^{-5}$ near-tie
between the elastic net and the lasso on \texttt{vol\_demand}. The gradient is
monotone in the $\ell_1$ weight: the more selection a penalty performs, the
worse it forecasts. There is no bucket --- not one --- on which concentrating
the fit into a subset of columns beats keeping every column and shrinking.

The Diebold--Mariano statistics sharpen the same verdict. On
\texttt{all\_features}, ridge reaches $t = -19.9$ against the incumbent while
the lasso manages $-13.2$; on \texttt{moments} the gap is starker still,
$-19.2$ against $-8.9$; on \texttt{sentiment} the lasso arm is barely
significant at all ($-2.1$). Because DM normalizes the mean loss differential
by its HAC standard error, ridge's larger $|t|$ at every level means its edge
over HAR is steadier bar to bar: the dense reader collects a small,
near-constant contribution from many columns and wins by a little on almost
every bar, while the selective reader's edge comes and goes with whether its
chosen subset happens to be the active one. One caveat travels with this
paragraph: every DM statistic in the table is computed against the shared
incumbent, a third model, so the ridge-minus-lasso differences themselves
carry no significance statement yet.

\paragraph{The negative control: shrinkage is only for wide designs.}
The \texttt{baseline} row is the experiment's control, and it fails in the
informative direction. When the only regressors are the HAR lags and calendar
features --- a low-dimensional, well-conditioned, entirely informative design
--- all three penalized arms are \emph{worse} than plain OLS: ridge $0.13445$
($t = +5.0$), elastic net $0.13471$ ($+7.5$), lasso $0.13475$ ($+7.0$), all
decisively significant. Shrinkage is a variance-reduction device; on a design
with no estimation variance worth reducing, the bias it induces is pure cost.
Penalized estimation earns its keep only once the design matrix contains a
large number of weak columns --- which is exactly what the exogenous buckets
supply, and exactly the regime the rest of this section characterizes. This
asymmetry --- the backbone wants essentially no shrinkage, the wide block
wants a great deal --- is the observation the two-block estimator of
Section~\ref{sec:dense_weak_twoblock} is built on.

\subsection{The Diagnosis: Dense, Not Sparse}
\label{sec:dense_weak_diagnosis}

Why does selection lose? The lasso's own solution path answers, because the
support it chooses tells us what $\ell_1$ selection is actually doing on this
design. On the full basis the lasso zeroes $304$ of $523$ exogenous columns
--- and $235$ of those, $77\%$, are dead or constant availability indicators.
The overwhelming bulk of the lasso's selection work is janitorial: removing
columns that carry no signal at all. That work is real but cheap --- $\ell_2$
shrinkage makes the same columns small without making them zero --- and it is
the \emph{only} selection that helps. Wherever the penalty bites into live
columns, it discards signal: on \texttt{implied\_vol}, a dense block of
all-informative VIX levels with no junk to remove, ridge's margin persists
because zeroing correlated informative columns is pure loss. Penalty
preference tracks the junk fraction of the design and nothing else.

The retained support says the same thing from the other side. The
elastic-net solution keeps approximately $120$ effective dimensions out of
$529$ features: the signal is not concentrated in five or ten strong columns
that a selector could find, it is spread across a hundred-plus weak ones that
a selector can only damage. And the spread is genuinely
coordinate-by-coordinate rather than a low-dimensional structure in disguise:
principal-component truncation fails outright, with five components retaining
$72\%$ of the feature variance while losing essentially all of the predictive
signal. Variance-directed rotation cannot find the signal because the signal
does not live in the high-variance directions. The precise statement, and we
choose the words carefully, is that the signal is \emph{sparse in the
coordinate basis only in the trivial sense that dead columns exist, and it is
not low-rank in the data's own covariance geometry}: no small subset of
columns and no small set of principal directions concentrates it. Dense but
weak is the only description left standing.

The same verdict reproduces on an entirely different basis. On the curated
kernel dictionary --- a compact design built to have no junk columns --- the
elastic net at light penalty ties ridge, the free prune it performs there
being confined to some sixty dead columns; the moment selection bites into
live columns it hurts, by $+0.005$ QLIKE at $p = 0.002$.

Selection that removes nothing important is free; selection that removes
anything else is harmful; and on a wide design there is no penalty level at
which the second does not accompany the first.

\subsection{The Two-Block Ridge}
\label{sec:dense_weak_twoblock}
% Endpoint: BlockRidge with block 1 = HAR(target), alpha=1; block 2 =
% HAR(all_features), alpha=100.

The head-to-head delivers a design rule --- \emph{never select, always
shrink} --- but Table~\ref{tab:lasso_vs_ridge} contains a second fact that a
single ridge penalty cannot honor. The \texttt{baseline} control row shows
that the persistence backbone is injured by the very shrinkage that the wide
exogenous block requires: the six HAR columns are few, well-conditioned, and
individually strong, so any penalty large enough to discipline hundreds of
weak columns over-shrinks the handful of strong ones, and any penalty small
enough to spare the backbone lets the wide block flood the fit with
estimation variance. One dial cannot serve two blocks whose optimal shrinkage
differs by orders of magnitude. The resolution is to stop pretending the
design is homogeneous.

The section's endpoint is therefore a \textbf{two-block ridge}: a single
jointly solved least-squares problem whose Tikhonov penalty is block-diagonal
rather than scalar. Block 1 is the HAR ladder of the target --- the
persistence backbone itself --- at $\alpha = 1$, shrunk only nominally. Block
2 is the same HAR averaging construction applied to \emph{every} exogenous
feature --- HAR-of-\texttt{all\_features}, the full wide basis --- at
$\alpha = 100$, shrunk two orders of magnitude harder. The two blocks are not
fit separately and stacked; the coefficients are estimated in one solve, so
the wide block corrects only what the backbone leaves unexplained and the
backbone is never distorted to accommodate it.
\pending{how the $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2) = (1, 100)$ pair was fixed ---

The estimator is the dense-but-weak diagnosis made operational, clause by
clause. Because the signal is dense, block 2 keeps all of its columns:
nothing is selected, and every weak column contributes its small, shrunken
share. Because the signal is weak, block 2 is shrunk hard: no individual
exogenous coefficient is trusted, and the fit aggregates many attenuated
contributions instead of amplifying any one. Because the backbone is neither
dense nor weak, block 1 is left nearly alone, so the incumbent of
Section~\ref{sec:data_prep} is effectively \emph{nested} inside the model:
zeroing block 2 recovers, up to the nominal $\alpha = 1$, the OLS--HAR
benchmark itself. The two-block ridge is thus the smallest estimator that
takes both lessons of Table~\ref{tab:lasso_vs_ridge} seriously at once ---
and it contains no selection step and no tuned dial: the penalties are fixed
constants, not optimized quantities.

The two-block ridge is this paper's spine, and the next two sections grow it
one block at a time: Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear_product} asks what
nonlinearity the trees find, distills it into an explicit product block, and
arrives at a three-block ridge; Section~\ref{sec:transmission} adds the
transmission block of factor lead--lag features and arrives at the final
four-block model. At every step the template is the one fixed here: keep
everything, shrink each block as hard as its density-and-weakness demands,
and select nothing.
